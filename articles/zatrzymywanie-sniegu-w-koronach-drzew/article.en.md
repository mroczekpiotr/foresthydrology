In mountain and upland catchments of the temperate zone, a large share of annual water resources originates from melting snow. How a forest handles snow — from the moment it falls to the spring melt — has a direct effect on how much water reaches a catchment, and when.

## Snow interception by tree canopies

As with rain, tree canopies capture part of the falling snow before it reaches the ground. This is called snow interception, and it is especially significant in coniferous forests with dense, evergreen foliage.

- Pine or spruce needles offer a large surface area on which snow can accumulate, particularly during snowfall near 0°C.
- Snow held in the canopy can partly sublimate — passing directly from solid to gas — which represents a permanent water loss for the catchment.
- The remaining intercepted snow eventually falls to the ground as clumps or drips down after melting.

## Differences between coniferous and deciduous forest

Canopy structure is decisive for how much snow is intercepted.

| Forest type | Typical snow interception | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Dense coniferous forest | High | Significant canopy sublimation; shade limits melt at the ground |
| Deciduous forest (leafless in winter) | Low | Snow reaches the ground largely unimpeded |
| Open clearing / clear-cut | Very low | High exposure to wind and solar radiation; faster melt |

## Effect on melt rate

Beyond the amount of snow retained, forests also affect the rate and timing of snowpack melt at the ground:

1. **Shading** — tree canopies reduce the solar radiation reaching the snow surface, slowing melt compared with open terrain.
2. **Wind shelter** — lower wind speeds beneath the canopy reduce heat advection, which otherwise accelerates melt in open areas.
3. **A longer snow season** — as a result, snow beneath a dense coniferous canopy can persist longer than in adjacent open areas, delaying and stretching out the melt pulse.

> Managing forest structure — for example through stand thinning — is considered a tool for regulating the amount and timing of meltwater release in catchments that supply reservoirs.

## Practical significance

Understanding canopy snow interception and melt processes matters for:

- forecasting the size and timing of snowmelt flood peaks,
- planning forest management in catchments feeding water reservoirs,
- assessing how climate change affects the seasonality of water availability in mountain regions, where melting snow is the primary source feeding rivers in spring and early summer.

## Summary

A forest is not a passive element of the snow landscape — it actively shapes the amount, spatial distribution, and rate of snow melt. These processes directly affect the hydrological regime of snowmelt-fed rivers, making them an important consideration for water-resource planning in mountain and upland regions.
