Water indices in remote sensing: NDWI, NDMI, MNDWI, AWEI and more

Piotr Mroczek · 4 September 2026
#RemoteSensing#NDWI#NDMI#Sentinel2#Water

Spectral indices are among the simplest and most useful tools in optical remote sensing. They transform reflectance in selected spectral bands into a normalized quantity that highlights a particular property. For hydrology, the key question is often whether an index is describing open water, vegetation water content, surface moisture, or some combination of these.

First: NDWI is not always the same NDWI

The abbreviation NDWI is used for two different indices. McFeeters (1996) proposed a green–NIR formulation for mapping open water. Gao (1996) proposed an NIR–SWIR formulation for vegetation water content; this latter formulation is commonly called NDMI. A review of water-monitoring methods explicitly notes this terminology problem.

IndexTypical formulaMain use
NDWI (McFeeters)(Green − NIR) / (Green + NIR)Open water mapping
NDMI / NDWI (Gao)(NIR − SWIR) / (NIR + SWIR)Vegetation moisture / canopy water content
MNDWI(Green − SWIR) / (Green + SWIR)Open water; often useful in built-up areas
AWEISeveral formulationsAutomated water extraction; suppressing shadows/background
WRI(Green + Red) / (NIR + SWIR)Water extraction
LSWI(NIR − SWIR) / (NIR + SWIR)Surface/vegetation water signal

How to choose an index?

For a lake, reservoir, river or wetland, start with an open-water index such as NDWI or MNDWI and validate it against imagery and field information. In urban or shadow-rich environments, MNDWI or AWEI can reduce some sources of confusion. For forest stands, NDMI is often more directly relevant because it responds to vegetation water content rather than simply delineating open water.

Sentinel-2 example

Sentinel-2 provides green, NIR and SWIR bands at resolutions useful for many landscape-scale applications. Band choice matters: the exact wavelength and spatial resolution should be stated in a reproducible workflow. Thresholds should not be treated as universal constants; they depend on sensor, season, atmosphere, land cover, turbidity, shadows and the question being asked.

Important limitation

No spectral index is a hydrological measurement by itself. An index is an observable proxy. If the goal is to infer soil moisture, groundwater conditions, drought stress or hydrological connectivity, combine spectral information with terrain, meteorological, soil, vegetation and/or field measurements.

Practical workflow

  1. Define the hydrological phenomenon you want to detect.
  2. Select bands and an index consistent with that phenomenon.
  3. Preprocess imagery appropriately.
  4. Calculate the index.
  5. Inspect spatial and temporal patterns.
  6. Validate with reference data.
  7. Only then define thresholds or build a classification/model.

For Forest Hydrology, the most interesting applications are likely to be combinations of NDMI/vegetation indices with DEM-derived terrain variables, soil data and hydrological modelling.
