An Introduction to Forest Hydrology

What forest hydrology studies, why forests are called the "sponges" of a catchment, and which processes govern the water cycle in forest ecosystems.

Published: August 10, 2026 2 min read

This article was translated automatically from Polish. If anything looks unclear, please contact the editors.

Forest hydrology is the field of science that studies how forests shape the movement of water through nature — from the moment a raindrop hits a tree canopy to the moment it reaches a stream, a river, or an aquifer. It draws on hydrology, forest ecology, soil science, and climatology.

The forest as a catchment "sponge"

Forests are often described as the landscape's natural sponges. The metaphor is simplified, but it captures three key functions of forest cover well:

  • Slowing water flow — tree canopies, litter, and root systems increase surface roughness and promote infiltration into the soil.
  • Storing water — thanks to their high organic-matter content, forest soils can hold far more water than agricultural or urbanized soils.
  • Releasing water gradually — stored water reaches streams slowly, which moderates both sudden flood peaks and shortages during drought.

Catchments with high forest cover tend to show a more even streamflow regime compared with agricultural or urbanized catchments of similar size.

Key hydrological processes in a forest

The water cycle in a forest ecosystem can be broken down into several interconnected processes.

  1. Interception — part of the precipitation is caught on the surface of leaves, needles, and bark, where some of it evaporates back into the atmosphere without ever reaching the ground.
  2. Stemflow and throughfall — water that does not evaporate runs down tree trunks or drips from the canopy onto the soil surface.
  3. Infiltration — thanks to forest soil structure and root systems, water moves down into the soil profile instead of running off the surface.
  4. Evapotranspiration — trees draw water from the soil through their roots and release it into the atmosphere through transpiration, a major component of a catchment's water balance.
  5. Subsurface and groundwater flow — water that has infiltrated the soil moves slowly toward streams, sustaining them between rainfall events.

Why this matters for water management

Understanding these processes has direct practical implications. Forest-management decisions — including deforestation, afforestation, or how clear-cuts are carried out — affect both the quantity and quality of water available in a catchment. Forest hydrology research provides data used, among other things, for:

ApplicationExample
Flood protectionAssessing how afforesting a catchment reduces flood peaks
Drinking-water supplyDelineating protection zones around water intakes in forested catchments
Drought adaptationModeling how forest structure affects water retention during rainfall shortages
Biodiversity conservationMaintaining an adequate flow regime in forest streams

Summary

Forest hydrology combines field observation, instrumental measurement, and mathematical modeling to describe how forests shape water availability and quality. In upcoming articles on this site, we will look more closely at individual processes — from snow interception by tree canopies to the role of riparian buffers in protecting water quality.
